# Prompt

Help me brainstorm “interactive experiences” that may be useful to a business, particularly as a marketing tool. In particular I’m thinking of something that’s sophisticated graphically, with drawn UI. Include ideas that are game-like, where a little mini game could be used for marketing or awareness. Also include ideas that are more practical where an “interactive experience” could help a user better understand if a business could solve a problem for them, provide something they would personally value

# Response

I think there’s a genuinely interesting product category here if you treat **“interactive experience” as a medium**, rather than as “a quiz” or “a game.”

The common idea is: instead of asking a visitor to *read* marketing material, give them something they can **manipulate, explore, play with, or personalize**, and let the experience itself communicate the value proposition.

I’d divide the space into several families.

### 1. “Show me my future” experiences

These may be the highest-value category for businesses because they're simultaneously useful and persuasive.

A landscaping company could offer **Design My Backyard**: start with a stylized yard, choose approximate size, sun/shade, priorities and budget, and watch the illustrated yard transform—patio appears, plants grow in, lighting turns on, etc.

A remodeling company could have **Build My Dream Kitchen**. Rather than a conventional configurator with dropdowns, users directly manipulate an illustrated kitchen: click cabinets, countertops, island, lighting, flooring. The experience gradually estimates budget and identifies a suitable service package.

Other examples:

- Solar installer → draw/approximate your roof, place panels, see estimated generation and savings.
- Interior designer → assemble a room and discover your design style.
- Pool company → visually configure a backyard/pool and get a rough project range.
- Wedding/event company → build a reception table or venue scene.
- Party rental company → **Build Your Party**: drag in tables, tents, bounce houses, balloon arches, food stations, etc.; see estimated guests accommodated and price.
- Hair salon → visual “style finder,” perhaps using illustrated heads rather than requiring a photo.
- Financial advisor → manipulate retirement age, house, travel, children, savings, etc. and watch a visual “future life” evolve.
- B2B software → construct your current workflow visually, then watch the product simplify it.

The important distinction from an ordinary calculator is that **the visualization is the primary interface**.

### 2. Diagnostic / “Could you help me?” experiences

This is perhaps the most broadly applicable practical category.

Imagine a cybersecurity company with an illustrated office building. The visitor answers questions by interacting with the scene:

> Where does your company keep customer data?

They click cloud, employee laptops, server room, SaaS apps, etc.

As they proceed, risks start appearing visually. At the end:

**Your Security Exposure: 68/100**

The office visualization highlights four vulnerabilities, and clicking each explains it.

The same pattern works surprisingly widely:

| Business | Interactive experience |
|---|---|
| Accountant | “Where is your business losing money?” |
| Lawyer | “How exposed is your business?” |
| Insurance | “Build your household and see your coverage gaps” |
| IT consultant | “Map your technology stack” |
| Marketing agency | “Diagnose your marketing funnel” |
| CRM | “Where are your leads falling through?” |
| HVAC | Interactive house comfort/efficiency audit |
| Personal trainer | “Build your week” and identify realistic exercise opportunities |
| Financial planner | Interactive financial health map |
| Realtor | “What kind of neighborhood/home actually fits you?” |

This becomes much more interesting than a Typeform-style questionnaire because **the questions can occur within a graphical model of the problem**.

### 3. Simulations

This category feels particularly promising for sophisticated B2B marketing.

Instead of saying:

> “Our product helps your sales team respond to leads faster.”

Let someone **experience the problem**.

For example, a CRM could create a 90-second **Sales Manager Challenge**. Leads start arriving. Emails come in. Existing customers need attention. Meetings happen. The visitor has to decide what to handle.

Things start falling through the cracks.

Then:

> You handled 11 of 18 opportunities.  
> Three high-value prospects never received a response.  
> Two follow-ups were forgotten.

Now replay the same scenario **with the product**. AI prioritizes things, automatically captures interactions, drafts responses, and reminds the salesperson.

That's a game, a product demo, and an argument for buying the product simultaneously.

This generalizes beautifully:

**“Do the job without us → now do it with us.”**

Payroll software could simulate payroll day. Security software could simulate an attack. Restaurant software could simulate Friday-night reservations. Logistics software could simulate deliveries. An AI receptionist product could simulate five customers calling simultaneously.

### 4. Mini-games that embody the brand

These don't necessarily qualify leads. Their purpose is **attention, memorability and sharing**.

The best ones aren't arbitrary games with a logo slapped on them. The game's mechanics relate to what the company does.

For a bakery:

**Cupcake Rush** — decorate cupcakes to match incoming orders before time runs out.

For a dentist:

**Plaque Attack** — clean an illustrated mouth while avoiding gums and finding hidden trouble spots.

For a moving company:

**Pack the Truck** — Tetris-like game fitting furniture into a moving truck.

For a landscaping company:

**Mow Master** — find the fastest route to mow a strangely shaped lawn.

For a pest-control company:

**Bug Hunt** — search an illustrated house for cleverly hidden termites, ants, roaches, etc.

For an accounting company:

**Tax Deduction Hunt** — explore a business scene and spot legitimate deductible expenses.

For a cybersecurity company:

**Spot the Phish** — an inbox fills with messages and you have 60 seconds to identify attacks.

For an insurance company:

**Disaster House** — things start going wrong with a house and you choose how to respond.

For a coffee shop:

**Barista Challenge** — memorize increasingly complicated drink orders.

For a party rental company:

**Save the Wedding** — increasingly absurd event disasters appear and the player has to solve them with available rental/event resources.

That last category could become quite funny and shareable.

### 5. “Find your…” experiences

These are descendants of the marketing quiz, but graphical UI could make them dramatically better.

Instead of:

> Question 3 of 8: What aesthetic do you prefer?  
> ○ Modern  
> ○ Traditional  
> ○ Rustic

Show an illustrated environment and let the visitor **make choices inside it**.

This produces things like:

- Find your interior-design style
- Find your perfect vacation
- Build your perfect wedding
- Find your coffee personality
- Find your ideal neighborhood
- Find your productivity style
- Find your ideal office
- Find your perfect event
- What's your company's AI maturity?
- What kind of salesperson are you?
- What's your marketing personality?

The result can become a highly designed **shareable card**.

> **You're a Warm Minimalist**
>
> Natural materials · muted colors · open spaces · plants · warm lighting

That gives the business both personalization and a viral/shareable artifact.

### 6. Exploratory “worlds”

I'd also experiment with experiences that feel almost like tiny websites inside an illustration.

Imagine an insurance agency's homepage is an isometric illustrated neighborhood. There are a house, apartment building, business, cars and family.

Instead of navigating menus, you explore:

**House → Homeowners insurance**  
**Car → Auto**  
**Storefront → Small-business insurance**  
**Family → Life insurance**

But it doesn't have to replace normal navigation. It could be a prominent:

**Explore what we can protect →**

That opens the graphical experience.

A university could have a campus. A financial institution could have a little town. A SaaS company could have an illustrated business. A travel company could have an island.

This starts approaching **interactive editorial illustration**, which I think is a particularly attractive aesthetic: sophisticated 2D/vector artwork rather than trying to imitate a video game.

### 7. Interactive stories

These are halfway between a game and a sales case study.

Instead of:

> “Acme increased conversions 42% using our product.”

you become the protagonist:

**It's Monday morning. You're the sales manager at Acme.**

An illustrated Slack message appears:

> CEO: We spent $40k on leads last month. Why did only 6% turn into meetings?

The visitor makes decisions.

The story branches for five minutes, demonstrating the problem and gradually introducing the solution.

This could be particularly good for products whose value proposition is difficult to explain in one sentence.

### 8. Interactive education

Sometimes the best marketing is simply giving someone a genuinely useful tool.

Examples:

A mortgage broker creates an interactive **Rent vs. Buy Journey** where users move through a visual timeline of the next ten years.

A solar installer lets users manipulate the sun, seasons, roof orientation, battery size and electricity consumption.

A financial advisor creates **Where Does Your Money Go?**—an animated household where spending visually flows into housing, taxes, transportation, food, retirement, etc.

An AI consultancy creates **What Could AI Do in My Company?** Visitors construct a little organization—sales, support, accounting, operations—and explore potential AI automations.

That last one is especially interesting because the output can become personalized:

> Based on your answers, we found **7 plausible AI automations**, representing approximately **31 employee-hours/week**.

And *that* becomes the lead-generation hook.

## One pattern I especially like: **Play → Discover → Personalize → Convert**

Rather than thinking separately about “marketing games” and “lead qualification tools,” I'd combine them.

A good experience might progress like this:

**1. Hook**  
Something immediately visual and interactive.

**2. Play/explore**  
The user manipulates something for 30–90 seconds.

**3. Personalize**  
The experience starts asking questions relevant to their actual situation.

**4. Reveal**  
It generates a useful personalized result.

**5. Action**  
“Want us to help you do this?”

That produces a much gentler conversion funnel than:

**Landing page → marketing copy → REQUEST DEMO.**

For example, a party/event company could offer:

**Plan My Party**

You start with an empty illustrated backyard.

Choose 30 / 60 / 100 guests.

Tables appear.

Add food, drinks, tent, dance area, balloons, kids' entertainment.

The environment changes as you manipulate it. It starts warning:

> ⚠ 80 guests probably won't fit comfortably under this tent.

Eventually:

> **Your party**
>
> 75 guests  
> 10 tables  
> 80 chairs  
> 20×40 tent  
> Balloon arch  
> Dance floor
>
> Estimated rental range: $1,850–$2,450

Then:

**Send this plan to Sarah's Party Rentals →**

That's not really a game, not really a form, not really a configurator, and not really a website. **“Interactive experience” actually seems like the right abstraction.**

## And AI makes this category much more interesting

I wouldn't necessarily put an AI chatbot front and center. Instead, AI can make the graphical experience *adaptive*.

Someone could type:

> “I'm having a graduation party for about 60 people in my backyard. Mostly adults but probably 10 kids. I'd like it to feel nice but not crazy expensive.”

The experience **constructs the initial scene**.

Then the visitor manipulates it graphically.

Or an AI might occasionally surface contextual suggestions:

> You have 60 guests but seating for 32. For this kind of casual event, 40–45 seats is usually sufficient. Want me to add 10 chairs?

This combination—**generative AI + structured graphical interaction**—could be much more compelling than either a conventional chatbot or conventional web form.

And importantly for businesses, the resulting structured state is incredibly useful. By the time someone says *contact me*, the business doesn't merely have:

> Bret — email@example.com — interested in party rentals

It has something closer to a **mini requirements document** describing exactly what the prospective customer wants.

I think there's potentially a product/platform concept hiding here: businesses describe what they sell and the customer decisions involved, while a system generates branded, sophisticated interactive experiences—ranging from playful mini-games to diagnostic tools, planners, simulations and visual configurators. The experience itself becomes both **content marketing and structured lead capture**.
